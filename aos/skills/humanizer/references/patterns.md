# Humanizer pattern catalog (§1-§26)

Loaded on demand by ../SKILL.md. Adapted from blader/humanizer v4.0.0 (MIT).

## A. Staging instead of stating (act on one sighting)

**§1 Not X but Y.** Watch for: not X but Y; not just/only/merely X, but Y; it's not X, it's Y; X rather than Y; the contrast split across sentences ("This does not mean X. It means Y."); a clipped negative tail (", no guessing"). The negative half names something no one claimed so the positive half sounds larger. State the point directly. Keep a contrast only when it corrects a belief the reader holds or both halves carry information.
> Before: It's not merely a song, it's a statement. / After: The heavy beat makes the song sound more aggressive.

**§2 One-line closers and dramatic fragments.** Watch for: a one-sentence paragraph restating the one before; "That is the real win."; "That distinction matters."; "Let that sink in."; the same closer after several sections; a sentence naming what an example just showed ("This shows the importance of..."); rows of fragments ("No aesthetic prior. No nostalgia."); ALL CAPS or every. single. word. Cut closers that repeat; keep one that adds a new fact or consequence. Merge fragments into one specific sentence.

**§3 Sayings that sound deep.** Watch for: the real question is, at its core, what really matters, fundamentally, the heart of the matter, X is the Y of Z, X becomes a trap, not a tool but a mirror, the language/currency/architecture of. Replace the aphorism with the specific claim.
> Before: Symmetry is the language of trust. / After: Symmetric layouts often feel more predictable to users.

**§4 Staged run-up.** Watch for: Let's dive in, let's break this down, here's what you need to know, without further ado, quick note, Honestly?, Look, Here's the thing, Real talk. Remove the run-up and make the point. "Honestly" inside a casual sentence is ordinary; the tell is a standalone opener before a routine claim.

**§5 Arguing with no one.** Watch for: This isn't about, I'm not saying, To be clear, Don't get me wrong, One might be tempted to, A tempting approach would be, You might think... but. Remove defenses against objections or options that appear nowhere else. Keep an objection the text attributes or answers in full, and an option a reader would actually weigh.

## B. Rhythm by rule

**§6 Forced triads.** Ideas arrive in threes to sound complete. Check each item adds a distinct idea; merge, develop the strongest, or vary the structure. Keep three when the meaning needs three.
> Before: Attendees can expect innovation, inspiration, and industry insights. / After: There's also time for informal networking between sessions.

**§7 Repeated sentence openings.** Several sentences in a row start with the same subject. Merge them or begin with the action. Deliberate rhythm ("She came. She saw. She conquered.") stays.

**§8 Dashes as the universal connector.** The final rewrite must not contain em dashes (—), en dashes used as dashes (–), or double hyphens (--) used as dashes, unless the writer's sample uses them; then match the sample's rate. Replace with a period, comma, colon, or parentheses, or rewrite. Leave number ranges, code, commands, paths, URLs, and product codes alone. One dash alone is *weak alone*; a text full of them is not.

**§9 Stacked qualifiers.** Watch for: could potentially, might arguably, it's also possible, in some cases it may. Keep a qualifier only when the source supports the doubt. Keep scope statements, legal and safety notices, and real corrections. *Weak alone.*
> Before: It could potentially possibly be argued that the policy might have some effect. / After: The policy may affect outcomes.

**§10 Hyphenated pairs everywhere.** Keep the hyphen before a noun (a high-quality report), drop it after (the report is high quality). Dictionary hyphenations (third-party, cross-functional) keep it. *Weak alone.*

**§11 Passive voice and missing subjects.** Use active voice when it makes the actor clearer ("No configuration file needed." becomes "You do not need a configuration file."). *Weak alone.*

## C. Inflation and borrowed authority (keep the fact, remove the dressing)

**§12 Overused AI words.** additionally, align with, bolstered, crucial, deep dive, delve, enduring, enhance, garner, gate/gating (figurative only), highlight (verb), interplay, intricate, key (adjective), landscape (abstract), meticulous, pivotal, quietly, robust (figurative only), showcase, tapestry, testament, underscore (verb), valuable, vibrant. Strongest in clusters. A formal word outside this list is not a tell by itself.

**§13 Inflated significance.** Watch for: stands as a testament, a pivotal moment, plays a key role, reflects a broader, lasting legacy, setting the stage for, evolving landscape; "Despite these challenges... continues to thrive"; stock "Challenges and outlook" sections; "the future looks bright", "exciting times ahead". Keep the fact, drop the significance, end on the last concrete fact. Use stated plans if the source has them.
> Before: established in 1989, marking a pivotal moment in the evolution of regional statistics. / After: established in 1989, part of a wider decentralization in Spain.

**§14 Vague connection.** Watch for: associated with, in connection with, linked to, tied to. Name the relationship (founded, leads, supplies). If you do not know it, keep the vague wording.

**§15 Shallow -ing phrases.** Watch for: highlighting, underscoring, ensuring, reflecting, symbolizing, contributing to, fostering, showcasing bolted onto a fact. Keep the -ing phrase only when the source supports what it claims.

**§16 Sales language.** Watch for: rich (figurative), profound, commitment to, nestled, in the heart of, groundbreaking, renowned, diverse array, breathtaking, stunning, world-class, cutting-edge, seamless. State what the thing is and does. In approved marketing copy, keep claims Legal or Marketing has signed off and trim only the inflation around them.

**§17 Borrowed authority.** Watch for: experts argue, observers have cited, industry reports, featured in [list of outlets], over N followers. Name the real source and what it said when known; otherwise cut the unsupported claim. A missing citation alone is not a tell.

**§18 Avoiding is, are, has.** Watch for: serves as, stands as, functions as, represents, boasts, features, offers. Use is, are, has.
> Before: Gallery 825 serves as the exhibition space and boasts over 3,000 square feet. / After: Gallery 825 is the exhibition space and has more than 3,000 square feet.

## D. Formatting by rule (the tell is decoration on every item)

**§19 Bold as decoration.** Remove bold without a reason. Turn a list where every item has a bold label and colon into prose when the labels carry no information.
> Before: - **Performance:** Performance has been enhanced through optimized algorithms. / After: The update adds faster algorithms.

**§20 Decorative headings.** Use sentence case, remove emojis and arrows (→) used as decoration, drop horizontal rules between every section, and do not repeat the document title as a top heading. A heading written for effect should name what the section holds.

**§21 Curly quotes.** Curly quotes where the target format uses straight quotes. Most editors auto-curl, so *weak alone*.

## E. Leftovers from the chat and the draft (remove outright)

**§22 Chatbot residue.** I hope this helps, Of course!, Certainly!, Great question!, You're absolutely right, Would you like..., let me know, here is a... Remove the wrapper, keep the content. Ordinary letter salutations and sign-offs stay.

**§23 Knowledge-limit disclaimers and guesses.** as of my last update, based on available information, not publicly available, maintains a low profile, likely [grew up/began], it is believed that. State what the source does not show, or cut the sentence. Never replace the gap with a guess.

**§24 Heading repeated in the first sentence.** Remove a one-line paragraph that restates its heading ("## Performance / Speed matters.").

**§25 Writing about the document.** Cut text describing what it replaced (outside change logs and release notes), how it was assembled ("compiled from", "anything unconfirmed is flagged"), or a layout the reader can see ("the table below compares"). Keep a source credit the reader can follow and a caveat that changes what the reader should do. *Weak alone* for a single instance.

## F. Writing for the wrong reader

**§26 Re-explaining what the reader knows.** Act only when you can see the surrounding conversation or the text plainly is a reply. A reply that restates the problem, walks through the diagnosis, and proves the plan before reaching the decision buries the point. Lead with the decision; keep only the fact the reader lacks and any link they need to act. Move diagnosis and proof to the ticket or document. If you cannot tell, ask or leave it.
